1. Overview

The project processes multi-source satellite data to compute the true, physically-representative average RGB color of distinct surface categories: oceans, forests, deserts, ice sheets, urban areas, and more, for each calendar month. The result is a structured dataset (earth_hues.json) mapping every surface category to a hex color per month, corrected for the spatial distortion inherent in geographic coordinate grids. The output is then used in D3 for visualizations.

2. Data Sources

Three primary datasets are utilized in this analysis:

Dataset Format Description
NASA Blue Marble (×12) GeoTIFF True-colour RGB surface reflectance composites at $\sim$10 km resolution, one per calendar month.
GEBCO 2025 GeoTIFF Global seabed and terrain elevation, stitched from a multi-tile mosaic.
MODIS MCD12C1 HDF IGBP land cover classification at 0.05° ($\sim$5.6 km per pixel), 17 discrete classes.

The Blue Marble composites provide the raw colour signal. GEBCO provides the elevation dimension needed to split ocean from inland water and to define the mountain mask. MODIS provides the categorical overlay that separates forests from grasslands, croplands from deserts, and so on.

3. Methodology

3.1 Reference Grid Establishment

All datasets are aligned to a common reference frame derived from the January Blue Marble composite: a global unprojected grid in EPSG:4326 (WGS84) with uniform 0.1° spacing.

$$\text{Grid} \in \mathbb{R}^{1800 \times 3600}, \quad \Delta\lambda = \Delta\phi = 0.1^\circ$$

This covers $[-90^\circ, 90^\circ] \times [-180^\circ, 180^\circ]$. Every subsequent dataset is aligned to this transform before any computation.

3.2 GEBCO Elevation Mosaic

GEBCO 2025 distributes global bathymetry as regional tiles at $\sim$450 m native resolution. Each tile is downsampled from disk via average resampling to match the reference grid:

$$\hat{e}_{i,j} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{i,j}|} \sum_{p \in \mathcal{P}_{i,j}} e_p$$

The resulting array $\mathbf{E} \in \mathbb{R}^{1800 \times 3600}$ stores signed elevation in metres, with negative values indicating depth below sea level.

3.3 Land Cover Reprojection

The MODIS MCD12C1 HDF encodes IGBP land surface classification. The file lacks a standard geotransform, so the code reprojects it explicitly. Nearest-neighbour resampling is required to prevent interpolation artifacts on discrete class integers.

$$L_{i,j} = L_{\text{src}}\!\left(T_{\text{src}}^{-1}\!\left(T_{\text{ref}}(i, j)\right)\right)$$

The resulting array $\mathbf{L} \in \mathbb{Z}^{1800 \times 3600}$ assigns each pixel one of 17 IGBP classes (0–16).

3.4 Latitude-Cosine Area Weighting

In an equirectangular grid, physical area shrinks toward the poles. Without correction, polar regions are overrepresented. Since longitude step width scales by the cosine of latitude, the relative area weight applied to each pixel is:

$$w(\phi) = \cos(\phi)$$

Where $\phi$ is the latitude of the given row in the grid.

3.5 Surface Category Masking

The mountain category uses a topographic roughness index ($\rho$) calculated from the DEM gradient, distinguishing rugged terrain from flat elevated plateaus.

Category Mask Condition
Oceans $L = 0 \;\wedge\; E < 0$
Fresh Water $L = 0 \;\wedge\; E \geq 0$
Snow and Ice $L = 15$
Deserts $L = 16$
Forests $L \in \{1\ldots5\}$
Grasslands $L \in \{8, 9, 10, 14\}$
Shrublands $L \in \{6, 7\}$
Croplands $L = 12$
Wetlands $L = 11$
Urban Areas $L = 13$
Mountains $E > 1000\,\text{m} \;\wedge\; \rho > 50$

3.6 Area-Weighted Mean Color Extraction

For each month $m$ and category $k$, the area-weighted mean for each color channel $c \in \{R, G, B\}$ is computed over the valid masked pixels $\mathcal{M}_{k}^{(m)}$:

$$\bar{c}_k^{(m)} = \frac{\sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{M}_{k}^{(m)}} w_{i}\; c_{i,j}}{\sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{M}_{k}^{(m)}} w_{i}}$$

Each channel mean is rounded, clamped to $[0,255]$, and encoded as a hexadecimal string.

4. Visualizations

Six charts show the dataset as a raw colour grid, channel-level decompositions, and seasonal brightness anomalies.

4.1 True Mean Earth Color

Each cell shows the area-weighted mean hex colour for a surface type and month.

Findings

The ocean row is the flattest in the grid: twelve months resolve to only three distinct values, all within one unit of #060a16. Seawater absorbs nearly all visible light at every wavelength, year-round.

Snow and Ice is equally static at the bright end, four distinct values around #ebeef0. Forests has the largest seasonal range of any land class: #52564f in March, darkening to #1e2a0f in September as Northern Hemisphere canopy closes, then back to #3d453a by December. It is also the only vegetation class whose multi-decade trend clears the two-satellite check in Section 6.

4.2 Earth's Annual Color Signature

The aggregate signals (total Earth, total land, and total ocean) across the full year.

Findings

Total Earth Mean runs from #595653 in March to #45423c in September, an $L^*$ range of 28.1 to 36.8. Area-weighted, Earth's surface is a mid grey that leans faintly warm, not blue. All twelve months are distinct; the seasonal signal is not rounding noise.

Carl Sagan called Earth a pale blue dot. He was describing the planet as seen through its atmosphere; this is the ground beneath it. Section 5 adds the atmosphere back.

4.3 Seasonal Color Drift from January

RGB Euclidean distance of each month's mean colour from the January baseline, per surface type.

Findings

Wetlands reaches 238 RGB units by August–September, against 147 for the next category. The signal is bimodal: boreal winter produces cool grey-green tones from frozen Arctic peatland, while Northern Hemisphere summer darkens as vegetation grows and open water replaces ice. Shrublands shows the second-largest drift, driven by drying in both hemispheres during mid-year months.

4.4 Seasonal Color Wheels

Each biome as a 12-segment wheel. The outer rim marks the four seasons; inner wedges show mean colour per month.

Findings

The Oceans wheel is a near-perfect monochrome disc. Snow and Ice is equally static but bright. Forests, Wetlands, and Shrublands show the widest contrast: lighter in boreal winter, darker in summer.

4.5 RGB Channel Decomposition

R, G, and B channel intensities (0–255) per surface type across twelve months.

Findings

For most land categories, the three channels track together, i.e. seasonal change is mainly a shift in brightness rather than hue. Shrublands are the clearest exception: from January to August, blue drops by 103 units compared with 66 for red. The larger drop in blue gives the summer minimum its warmer brown tone, consistent with dried-out vegetation.

4.6 Seasonal Brightness Anomaly

Deviation of monthly perceptual luminance from the annual mean for each surface type.

Findings

Wetlands has the largest range: January–May run +16.8 to +20.2 pp above the annual mean, August–October drop to −27.7 to −33.9 pp, a total span of 54 points. Shrublands has the second-largest span. Grasslands and Croplands follow similar curves, shaped by Northern Hemisphere growing seasons. Urban Areas are nearly flat.

4.7 Five Ways to Average a Planet

There is no single correct way to reduce millions of pixels to one colour. Each row shows the same January category under five estimators, and the bar is the perceptual distance between the naive answer and the physically correct estimates.

Findings

Snow and Ice moves 0.3 ΔE₀₀ between methods; Oceans moves 0.6. Both are internally uniform, so averaging method matters little. Total Earth moves 15.2, from a near-black #28292d to a mid grey #565552.

The size of the shift tracks the internal contrast of each category. Averaging gamma-encoded values underestimates radiance in proportion to that contrast — Jensen's inequality applied to the sRGB transfer curve. The next figure measures the contrast directly.

4.8 Does One Colour Describe a Category?

Each box spans the interquartile range of pixel lightness within a category, whiskers reach the 5th and 95th percentiles, and the rule inside marks the median. Boxes are filled with the category's own mean colour, so a wide box is a colour that describes its category poorly.

Findings

Only Oceans and Snow and Ice have low spreads, 1.4 and 3.2 respectively. Fresh Water is the highest at 31.2, spanning $L^*$ 1.4 to 95.5: frozen lakes and open tropical water fall in the same class with no meaningful midpoint.

Across all 168 category-months, spread correlates with the gamma shift in the previous figure at $r = 0.940$. The error from averaging in the wrong colour space scales with the variance inside the category, as the concavity argument predicts.

5. Earth from Space

The colours above are surface colours. Blue Marble is atmospherically corrected and cloud-cleared, so it records what the ground reflects, not what a distant observer sees. Two layers sit in between.

The first is Rayleigh scattering. Optical depth goes as $\lambda^{-4}$, so the atmosphere scatters blue roughly four times more strongly than red, and that scattered light is added along every viewing path. On a bright surface the effect is negligible; on a dark one it becomes the dominant signal. The second layer is cloud, which covers about two thirds of the planet at any moment.

5.1 The Map

Every pixel is painted with the mean colour of the category it belongs to, so the map shows the planet reduced to fourteen colours and nothing else. It opens on the view from space, with the atmosphere in place. Drag the slider to move through the year, and switch layers to strip the air away or bring in the cloud deck.

Findings

The atmosphere shifts the ocean from #060a16 to #30415c; the Sahara barely moves, from #ae9675 to #ad9781. Earth looks blue because most of its surface is dark enough for scattered blue light to dominate, not because seawater is blue.

The cloud layer makes the intertropical convergence zone, the Southern Ocean storm track, and the clear subtropical belts visible. Averaged within a category, that spatial structure reduces to a single grey — at two-thirds cloud cover the surface underneath is no longer the main signal. The gap between the three layers is largest where the surface is darkest.

5.2 Checking the Model Against a Camera

The model has one fitted parameter: an effective cloud reflectance, set so that whole-Earth albedo matches the observed value. That fit constrains brightness but not colour balance, so the blue-to-red ratio is an independent prediction. DSCOVR EPIC photographs the full sunlit disk from a million miles away and provides the test.

Findings

The bare surface leans red at 0.907. Atmosphere alone pushes the ratio to 1.448. With cloud the model gives 1.026, against 1.041 from the camera — within 0.003 in every channel, on a quantity that was not fitted.

The seasonal cycle is less accurate. Model and observation correlate at $r = 0.644$ across eleven months, brightest in December and dimmest around August, but the modelled amplitude is about an eighth of the observed one. Three cloud days per month is thin sampling, and EPIC's published frames carry a display tone curve, so the two amplitudes are not directly comparable.

6. Two Decades of Change

Blue Marble is a single climatological year, so it cannot say whether anything is changing. To ask that, the same measurement is repeated on 276 eight-day MODIS composites spanning 2001 to 2024, six per year, from two satellites: Terra and Aqua.

Both instruments observe the same planet and are calibrated independently, so the second acts as a control. Any trend that shows up on only one satellite is instrument drift, not the surface.

6.1 Two Satellites, One Planet

Each row is a surface category. The filled dot is the trend Terra reports, the hollow dot is Aqua, and the line between them is the disagreement. Two separate things are marked, and they are not the same test. The shaded band spans twice the drift measured over deserts, which are radiometrically stable, so a category whose dots fall inside it has no trend worth claiming and a bold name means both satellites put it outside. The right-hand column is the gap between the two satellites, daggered where that gap alone exceeds the desert floor.

Findings

Every land category falls within the band, most with the two dots nearly touching. Total Land differs by 0.008 L* per decade between the two satellites — small enough to trust.

Oceans is outside the band by a wide margin. Terra reports +0.404 L* per decade; Aqua reports $-$0.234 with no significance. The gap of 0.639 is eighteen times the 0.036 that separates the two over deserts. Terra is known to drift on dark targets. A project using one satellite would have reported ocean brightening as a real trend.

6.2 Annual Lightness by Category

Mean lightness per year, Terra solid and Aqua dashed. Categories in bold clear the desert noise floor on both instruments. Each panel is scaled to its own range, so compare shapes rather than heights.

Findings

Two categories clear the control on both satellites: Forests, darkening at $-$0.49 (Terra) and $-$0.56 (Aqua) L* per decade, and Urban Areas at $-$0.54 and $-$0.89. Forest darkening is consistent with denser canopy absorbing more visible light, the optical counterpart of the greening seen in vegetation indices.

Total Land is significant on both sensors and still not claimable: its trend of $-$0.19 sits within 0.01 of the desert control itself, so it cannot be separated from shared calibration drift. Statistical significance and physical meaning are not the same test.

7. Notes & Limitations

Resampling strategy

Average resampling preserves radiometric accuracy for continuous GEBCO elevation. Nearest-neighbour is reserved for categorical MODIS land cover to prevent class interpolation artifacts.

Polar weighting

Cosine weighting suppresses polar pixels toward zero without hard-excluding them. Contribution diminishes smoothly as $\phi \to \pm 90^\circ$.

Mountain definition

The mountain mask requires elevation above 1000 m and roughness $\rho > 50$, excluding flat elevated plateaus. It is non-exclusive — pixels may belong simultaneously to Mountains and another category.

Urban Areas anomaly

At MODIS 0.05° resolution, no pixel resolves a pure urban surface. The IGBP classifier systematically misclassifies dense Asian cities. The urban class therefore describes low-density suburban fabric, not global urban land.

Colour space

All averages are computed on gamma-compressed values as stored in the GeoTIFFs, not on physically linear light. The perceptual difference is negligible for broad macro-statistics.
