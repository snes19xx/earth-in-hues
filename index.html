1. Overview

The project processes multi-source satellite data to compute the true, physically-representative average RGB color of distinct surface categories: oceans, forests, deserts, ice sheets, urban areas, and more, for each calendar month. The result is a structured dataset (earth_hues.json) mapping every surface category to a hex color per month, corrected for the spatial distortion inherent in geographic coordinate grids. The output is then used in D3 for visualizations.

2. Data Sources

Three primary datasets are utilized in this analysis:

Dataset Format Description
NASA Blue Marble (×12) GeoTIFF True-colour RGB surface reflectance composites at ~10 km resolution, one per calendar month.
GEBCO 2025 GeoTIFF Global seabed and terrain elevation, stitched from a multi-tile mosaic.
MODIS MCD12C1 HDF IGBP land cover classification at 0.05° (~5.6 km per pixel), 17 discrete classes.

The Blue Marble composites provide the raw colour signal. GEBCO provides the elevation dimension needed to split ocean from inland water and to define the mountain mask. MODIS provides the categorical overlay that separates forests from grasslands, croplands from deserts, and so on.

3. Methodology

3.1 Reference Grid Establishment

All datasets are aligned to a common reference frame derived from the January Blue Marble composite: a global unprojected grid in EPSG:4326 (WGS84) with uniform 0.1° spacing.

$$\text{Grid} \in \mathbb{R}^{1800 \times 3600}, \quad \Delta\lambda = \Delta\phi = 0.1^\circ$$

This covers $[-90^\circ, 90^\circ] \times [-180^\circ, 180^\circ]$. Every subsequent dataset is aligned to this transform before any computation.

3.2 GEBCO Elevation Mosaic

GEBCO 2025 distributes global bathymetry as regional tiles at ~450 m native resolution. Each tile is downsampled from disk via average resampling to match the reference grid:

$$\hat{e}_{i,j} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{i,j}|} \sum_{p \in \mathcal{P}_{i,j}} e_p$$

The resulting array $\mathbf{E} \in \mathbb{R}^{1800 \times 3600}$ stores signed elevation in metres, with negative values indicating depth below sea level.

3.3 Land Cover Reprojection

The MODIS MCD12C1 HDF encodes IGBP land surface classification. The file lacks a standard geotransform, so reprojection is handled explicitly. Nearest-neighbour resampling is required to prevent interpolation artifacts on discrete class integers.

$$L_{i,j} = L_{\text{src}}\!\left(T_{\text{src}}^{-1}\!\left(T_{\text{ref}}(i, j)\right)\right)$$

The resulting array $\mathbf{L} \in \mathbb{Z}^{1800 \times 3600}$ assigns each pixel one of 17 IGBP classes (0–16).

3.4 Latitude-Cosine Area Weighting

In an equirectangular grid, physical area shrinks dramatically toward the poles. Without correction, polar regions are massively overrepresented. Since longitude step width scales by the cosine of latitude, the relative area weight applied to each pixel is:

$$w(\phi) = \cos(\phi)$$

Where $\phi$ is the latitude of the given row in the grid.

3.5 Surface Category Masking

The mountain category utilizes a topographic roughness index ($\rho$) calculated from the DEM gradient, distinguishing rugged terrain from flat elevated plateaus.

Category Mask Condition
Oceans $L = 0 \;\wedge\; E < 0$
Fresh Water $L = 0 \;\wedge\; E \geq 0$
Snow and Ice $L = 15$
Deserts $L = 16$
Forests $L \in \{1\ldots5\}$
Grasslands $L \in \{8, 9, 10, 14\}$
Shrublands $L \in \{6, 7\}$
Croplands $L = 12$
Wetlands $L = 11$
Urban Areas $L = 13$
Mountains $E > 1000\,\text{m} \;\wedge\; \rho > 50$

3.6 Area-Weighted Mean Color Extraction

For each month $m$ and category $k$, the area-weighted mean for each color channel $c \in \{R, G, B\}$ is computed over the valid masked pixels $\mathcal{M}_{k}^{(m)}$:

$$\bar{c}_k^{(m)} = \frac{\sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{M}_{k}^{(m)}} w_{i}\; c_{i,j}}{\sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{M}_{k}^{(m)}} w_{i}}$$

Channel means are rounded, clamped to $[0,255]$, and encoded as standard hexadecimal strings.

4. Visualizations

Six charts explore the dataset from different angles: from a raw colour grid to channel-level decompositions and seasonal brightness anomalies.

4.1 True Mean Earth Color

Each cell shows the area-weighted mean hex colour for a surface type and month.

Findings

The ocean row is immediately legible: twelve identical squares of very deep navy (#030614), confirming that total ocean mean luminance is essentially constant. The ocean absorbs nearly all incident visible radiation at every wavelength.

Snow and Ice shows the opposite extreme — consistently the brightest row, near-white all year, with a barely perceptible warm shift in Northern Hemisphere summer as Arctic melt ponds and older snow reduce overall albedo. The Forests row traces a clear arc: warm dark-olive in boreal winter, deepening to near-black (#1b290d) in July–September, then recovering.

4.2 Earth's Annual Color Signature

The aggregate signals (total Earth, total land, and total ocean) across the full year.

Findings

Carl Sagan famously described Earth as a "pale blue dot," and it holds at interplanetary distance, where atmospheric scattering dominates the signal. The surface data differs. The Total Earth Mean sits around #242528 in summer and #28292d in winter — a near-neutral very dark grey with only a marginal blue bias. Area-weighted, Earth's surface is not pale and not meaningfully blue; the surface mean is simply dark.

4.3 Seasonal Color Drift from January

RGB Euclidean distance of each month's mean colour from the January baseline, per surface type.

Findings

Wetlands dominates by a wide margin, exceeding 200 RGB units by August–September. This is a bimodal signal: boreal winter produces cool grey-green tones from frozen Arctic peatland pixels, while Northern Hemisphere summer brings dramatic darkening as vegetation growth and open water reflectance take over. Shrublands shows the second-largest drift, capturing the double-hemisphere drying signal during mid-year months.

4.4 Seasonal Color Wheels

Each biome as a 12-segment wheel. The outer rim marks the four seasons; inner wedges show mean colour per month.

Findings

The Oceans wheel is a near-perfect monochrome disc. Snow and Ice is equally static but bright. Forests and Wetlands show the most visually striking wheels: a clear split between the lighter boreal-winter half and the darker summer half.

4.5 RGB Channel Decomposition

R, G, and B channel intensities (0–255) per surface type across twelve months.

Findings

For most land categories, all three channels track together — seasonal change is primarily a brightness modulation, not a hue shift. Shrublands is the clearest exception: in July–August red and green fall more steeply than blue, giving the summer minimum its warm brown character consistent with dry senesced vegetation.

4.6 Seasonal Brightness Anomaly

Deviation of monthly perceptual luminance from the annual mean for each surface type.

Findings

Wetlands commands the heatmap: January–May run strongly positive (+16 to +18 pp), while August–October plunge to −27 to −29 pp. Shrublands shows the second-sharpest contrast. Grasslands and Croplands show similar shapes, heavily driven by Northern Hemisphere agricultural cycles. Urban Areas are nearly flat throughout.

5. Notes & Limitations

Resampling strategy

Average resampling preserves radiometric accuracy for continuous GEBCO elevation. Nearest-neighbour is reserved for categorical MODIS land cover to prevent class interpolation artifacts.

Polar weighting

Cosine weighting suppresses polar pixels toward zero without hard-excluding them. Contribution diminishes smoothly as $\phi \to \pm 90^\circ$.

Mountain definition

The mountain mask requires elevation above 1000 m and roughness $\rho > 50$, excluding flat elevated plateaus. It is non-exclusive — pixels may belong simultaneously to Mountains and another category.

Urban Areas anomaly

At MODIS 0.05° resolution, no pixel resolves a pure urban surface. The IGBP classifier systematically misclassifies dense Asian cities. The urban class therefore describes low-density suburban fabric, not global urban land.

Colour space

All averages are computed on gamma-compressed values as stored in the GeoTIFFs, not on physically linear light. The perceptual difference is negligible for broad macro-statistics.
